Operational improvement

When the work needs simplifying, not more software.

A new system can make good work easier. It can also make a confused process faster, more expensive and harder to change.

By Geordie Lindsay Russell8 August 2026Six minute read

When a business grows, operational pressure often appears as a technology request. The team asks for a new system because orders are being missed, reporting takes too long, information is copied between tools or too much depends on one person.

Those symptoms are real. But they do not prove that software is the first problem to solve.

Across product, customer, technology and operations work, the pattern is consistent: the best result comes from understanding the work before selecting the tool. These five questions help.

What outcome actually matters?

Describe the business result without naming a system. It might be fewer missed orders, faster invoicing, reliable stock information or less owner intervention.

If the outcome cannot be stated clearly, comparing software will create more noise. A useful measure also makes later decisions easier because you can test whether the change worked.

Where does the work break?

Follow one real piece of work from beginning to end. Look for waiting, repeated entry, unclear ownership, missing information and decisions that return to the owner.

The constraint often sits between teams or tools, not inside one of them. A system purchased for one department may move the problem rather than resolve it.

Do not automate the diagram. Observe the work.

Is the process consistent enough?

Automation needs a dependable trigger, clear rules, known exceptions and an accountable owner. If five people complete the same task in five different ways, the first improvement may be agreeing the simplest sensible method.

This does not mean writing a large procedure manual. It means making the important decisions visible before encoding them into software or an AI workflow.

Can existing tools do more?

Many businesses already pay for useful capability they do not use. Before adding another platform, check whether the current tools can remove duplicate entry, improve visibility or connect the handover.

Keeping what you have is a valid recommendation when it produces the outcome with less cost and disruption.

What is the smallest useful change?

A contained improvement creates evidence. Trial one workflow, one customer journey or one reporting need. Confirm that people can use it and that the expected benefit appears before expanding.

This protects the business from a large commitment based on assumptions. It also reveals the exceptions and adoption needs that a sales demonstration rarely shows.

When software is justified

Software becomes a stronger option when the outcome is clear, the work is understood, ownership is agreed and the expected value is proportionate to the cost and change involved.

The right question is not whether a tool is impressive. It is whether the tool makes the operation simpler, clearer or more reliable.
